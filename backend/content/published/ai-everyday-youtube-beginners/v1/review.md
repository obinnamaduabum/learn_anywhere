## English lessons and answer keys

## 1. Describe AI, learning and language models

## Start with a useful question

Before choosing an AI tool, describe what you want to accomplish. What information will you give it? What result do you need? How will you check that result? These questions are useful even when you have only paper and a pen. In this lesson, all situations and records are fictional. You will practise describing tasks, not test the performance of an actual product. Keep your notes because they will become part of a small project later in the course.

Imagine a library preparing a weekend event. One worker adds the numbers of books returned today. Another estimates how many books might be returned tomorrow. A third writes a short invitation. The first task can use a fixed calculation. The second needs an estimate about an unknown result. The third produces new text. Calling every task AI would hide these useful differences. Begin with the task, then decide whether a simple method already meets the need.

## What a learned model does

Machine learning uses data to train a model to make predictions or generate content. In supervised learning, examples include known answers. Unsupervised learning seeks patterns without supplied answers. Generative AI creates content from learned patterns. A model is different from a physical device: it describes learned relationships used to produce an output. These descriptions help you ask sensible questions about a system without assuming that it thinks or understands as a person does.

Write three headings in your notebook: fixed calculation, prediction and generation. Under the first, put adding known book counts. Under the second, put estimating tomorrow's returns. Under the third, put drafting an invitation. Now invent one more library task for each heading. For each task, describe its input and output. If you are unsure how a real product works, write that uncertainty rather than deciding that its name proves it uses machine learning.

## Work through a prediction example

Suppose three invented delivery records show that distances of 1, 2 and 3 kilometres took 10, 15 and 20 minutes. Distance could be an input feature and delivery time the answer to predict. On paper, propose a rule for estimating the time for 4 kilometres. Clearly label your answer as an estimate. Then suppose the actual observed time was 28 minutes. Compare your estimate with the observation and describe the difference.

The tidy sequence makes this example easy to discuss. It does not establish a dependable rule for real deliveries. Add an invented delay to one record and see whether your proposed rule still describes every row. Next, imagine that every training record came from dry weather but the intended use includes heavy rain. Write a question you would need to investigate before relying on the model. This is practice in noticing limits, not evidence about weather or transport in a particular country.

## Understand language generation

A large language model learns patterns in text and predicts sequences of tokens. Tokens are pieces of text and are not necessarily complete words. Generating a plausible continuation is different from checking a claim against trustworthy evidence. A response can sound confident while containing an unsupported detail. You can investigate that difference without using a live chatbot: compare a supplied fact sheet with a deliberately imperfect draft.

Use these fictional facts: the library event is on Saturday, it begins at 10:00, and entry is free. Write a draft saying it is on Sunday at 10:00 and that snacks are included. Underline the parts supported by the fact sheet. Circle the conflicting day and the unsupported snack claim. Rewrite the message using only the supplied facts. Explain why a smooth writing style would not make Sunday correct or establish that snacks will be available.

## Separate three kinds of information

On a new page, make three columns called observed, estimated and missing. An observed entry is something supplied in your exercise, such as the recorded 28-minute delivery. An estimated entry is a proposed prediction, such as your time for 4 kilometres. A missing entry is something the evidence does not provide, such as the event venue. Keep these categories separate when you write your final answer.

Give yourself five short statements about the invented library. Include two facts from the source sheet, one prediction, one unsupported addition and one question. Assign each to a category and explain your choices. A question is a useful way to handle missing information. It allows the task to continue without disguising a guess as a fact. If a reader cannot tell which statements were supplied and which were estimated, revise the wording.

## Try a different task independently

Imagine a small book exchange that wants to organise returned books by topic. You are given five fictional descriptions: a cooking guide, a travel diary, a garden handbook, a collection of poems and a recipe booklet. Choose a simple sorting rule yourself. Then describe how a system trained on labelled examples would differ from your rule. You do not need to design its mathematics. Focus on who supplied the categories and what evidence would support a new classification.

Add a sixth description that is ambiguous, such as a travel diary containing recipes. Write two possible categories and explain what information would help you choose. Do not force a definite answer merely because your table has a space to fill. This activity introduces the habit of checking difficult cases, which will be useful when you review text, presentations and media plans in later lessons.

## Recap and keep evidence

Complete this sentence three ways: this task receives something and produces something. Use one calculation example, one prediction and one generation example. Next, explain the delivery estimate and invitation correction to a partner, or write the explanation for yourself. Include an observed result, a proposed estimate and a detail you still do not know. Keep your task list, delivery table, corrected invitation and ambiguous book example together. These records demonstrate your reasoning. They do not claim that you have trained, deployed or certified a working AI system.

## Check whether a method fits a new situation

Imagine a fictional cupboard containing ten labelled storage boxes. A fixed list records which box holds which item. A worker wants to retrieve an item whose label is already known. Begin with the list: this task does not require a prediction simply because it happens at work. Now change the task. The worker wants to estimate which items will be requested next week. That question involves an unknown result, so describe what earlier request records would be useful and how an estimate could later be checked.

Write the two tasks beside each other. In the first, the result can be found in supplied records. In the second, it is a proposed future answer. Add a third task that asks for a friendly reminder to return borrowed items. This produces a message, which still needs checking against the current return policy. Your table should describe the intended output rather than use the word smart as a substitute for an explanation.

For independent practice, create two invented records showing different request patterns. Do not combine them into a reliable forecast just because both are numbers. State which situations they represent and what you would need before using a prediction elsewhere. You can also propose a simple baseline, such as reviewing existing records manually, to clarify what a new method would need to improve.

Keep the task comparison as evidence. A useful reviewer should be able to follow your decisions from the inputs, purpose and proposed checks. The exercise demonstrates how to choose a starting method and identify unanswered questions, without making a claim that you have measured a real system.

### Explained version

Start by asking what goes in, what should come out and how you will check it. Adding known book counts can use a fixed rule. Predicting tomorrow's count needs an estimate. Drafting an invitation creates text.

Machine learning trains a model using examples. Supervised examples contain known answers; unsupervised learning looks for patterns without supplied answers. Generative models produce content from learned patterns. A language model works with pieces of text called tokens. A natural-sounding answer is not proof that its claims are true.

Practise with the fictional library event: Saturday, 10:00, free entry. If a draft says Sunday and includes snacks, change the day and remove the snack claim. Those corrections come from the fact sheet, not from the writer's confidence.

For prediction practice, use the three delivery records and clearly label your estimate for a new distance. Compare it with the supplied observed time of 28 minutes. Then add a difficult case and describe what you still need to know. Keep calculations, predictions and missing details separate.

Your evidence is a task list, a small table and a corrected invitation. The goal is to reason clearly about a task and its limits. You do not need a live AI tool or a claim that your paper rule works in every real situation.

### Questions

Which task needs an estimate rather than an exact calculation?
1. Predict tomorrow's number of book returns.
2. Add today's recorded returns.
3. Copy the supplied event time.
4. Sort a supplied list alphabetically.

Answer: 1. Tomorrow's count is unknown. Adding or copying known values does not estimate a future outcome.

The fact sheet says Saturday, but a fluent draft says Sunday. What should you do?
1. Trust Sunday because the wording is confident.
2. Correct the day using the fact sheet.
3. Invent a third day.
4. Assume both days are equivalent.

Answer: 2. The evidence determines the correction. Natural language does not establish factual accuracy.

A book description fits two categories. What is the most useful next step?
1. Claim certainty because a table requires one entry.
2. Delete all the source descriptions.
3. State the ambiguity and identify what information would help.
4. Treat the model's name as proof.

Answer: 3. A difficult case may require more information. Filling a table is not a reason to present a guess as certain.

## 2. Write prompts and compare assistant responses

## Describe the work before opening a tool

A prompt is a request describing what you want a tool to do. Useful requests can include instructions, source material, context and an example of the desired result. In this lesson, you will write prompts and responses on paper. You do not need a ChatGPT or Gemini account, a subscription or an internet connection to complete the activities. The assistant names connect the lesson to the playlist topics; the required learning is how to specify a task and check its result.

Imagine that you are helping a fictional library announce a reading session. Your fact sheet says Saturday, 10:00, free entry, and registration is optional. It supplies no venue and no organiser's name. Start by writing the intended reader: an adult learner receiving a short notice. Then write the action you want that reader to understand. This purpose will help you decide what information belongs in the message and what would be an unnecessary addition.

## Build a request with observable requirements

Use this original paper prompt: write one short paragraph for adult learners using only the supplied event facts; keep the wording plain; include the day, time, entry cost and registration condition; do not add a venue; identify any missing detail needed before distribution. You can assess each requirement by inspecting a proposed answer. A request such as make it good is harder to check because different readers may mean different things by good.

Copy the prompt and number its requirements. Leave space beside each number for evidence from the response. You are creating a review plan at the same time as the request. If you later revise the prompt, keep the earlier version rather than erasing it. The comparison will show which ambiguity you resolved and whether your new instruction addressed the actual problem.

## Review two invented responses

Write a first response that follows the fact sheet: the reading session is on Saturday at 10:00; entry is free; registration is optional. Write a second response that adds a main hall and says everyone must register. Compare them using your numbered requirements. The second response adds an unsupported venue and changes an optional action into a requirement. Identify both issues before judging which message sounds more friendly.

Now revise the second response without supplying new facts. If a venue is necessary for distribution, write a question asking the organiser to provide it. Keep that question separate from the notice so it cannot be mistaken for a confirmed detail. Explain why inventing a plausible hall name would make the message look more complete while making its evidence weaker.

## Use context and examples carefully

Context helps describe the reader and situation. An example can show the desired format or style. Neither should quietly change the facts. For practice, add a style example that uses short sentences but describes a different fictional event. Mark the example as a style example, not as the source of the new event's details. Then inspect a mock response that mistakenly copies its day or venue.

Write a better instruction clarifying that the sample demonstrates wording only. Create another response and check it against the actual fact sheet. This exercise teaches you to separate the material that may be transformed from the material that merely illustrates a format. You can use the same approach when requesting a summary, a presentation outline or a voice script.

## Understand assistant choices without promising identical features

ChatGPT is a conversational AI product, while Gemini is another family of assistant products covered by the playlist. Products, accounts and features can change. This course does not require a particular interface, button or paid feature. When comparing any two assistants, ask whether they can handle your intended task and whether you can check the output. A brand name is not evidence that a response is correct.

For a paper comparison, call your mock systems Assistant One and Assistant Two. Give both the same prompt and write different invented outputs. Evaluate them with the same criteria: factual agreement, required information, format and unsupported additions. Do not claim that these mock outputs measure the real products. The exercise demonstrates a fair comparison method rather than ranking actual services.

## Keep unnecessary personal information out of practice

Use invented names and event records in this lesson. You can learn to write a request without supplying learner contact details, private messages or a real person's history. Before optional future use of a real service, inspect its current data settings and terms. For the required activity, the simplest choice is to use only the fictional information already provided. This keeps the work easy to share with a reviewer.

Make a two-part worksheet. In the first part, list the information needed for the task. In the second, list information that is irrelevant to that task. For a reading-session notice, the public day and time belong in the first part. A learner's private medical history belongs in neither the public fact sheet nor the message. Explain your decision in terms of what the task actually needs.

## Revise a prompt independently

Choose a new fictional task: shorten a notice, reorganise a timetable or turn three facts into a checklist. Write the source information, intended reader and output requirements. Produce one acceptable answer and one answer that breaks a requirement. Ask a partner to review them using only your prompt, or review them yourself after setting the page aside briefly. If the review criteria are unclear, improve the prompt.

Give the exercise a manageable boundary. For example, request three checklist items rather than an entire handbook. Say whether uncertain details should be omitted or returned as questions. Compare the first and revised prompt and describe which wording became more precise. A longer request is not automatically better; every instruction should help someone produce or evaluate the intended result.

## Recap and demonstrate the review

Keep your fact sheet, numbered prompt, two mock answers, correction notes and final version. Explain the difference between creating instructions and verifying an output. State one requirement that you can check directly and one missing fact that still needs an organiser. You have practised prompting as part of a complete workflow: define the task, supply evidence, request a result and inspect it. The paper comparison does not establish a real assistant's accuracy, and completing it does not earn a provider credential.

## Change style without changing the task

Use a second fictional fact sheet: an organiser is collecting up to two suggested reading topics from each visitor; suggestions are optional; no deadline is supplied. Write one response for a notice board and another for a brief conversation. The two formats can differ in tone, but both should preserve the optional condition and the maximum number of suggestions. Neither should invent a closing date.

Now imagine that you ask for a more enthusiastic tone. Create a mock revision that says everyone must submit two topics by Friday. The revision has done more than change style. Mark each factual change and write a new instruction requesting lively wording while preserving every supplied condition. Explain how your revised instruction makes the error easier to identify.

Separate improvements to the prompt from corrections to an answer. A clearer prompt may reduce ambiguity, while your review still needs to catch any unsupported addition in the particular output. Do not assume that a revised request guarantees the next answer will be correct. Keep the before-and-after answers and a list of requirements so another reader can repeat your comparison.

Finally, decide what you would ask the organiser if a deadline were necessary for distribution. Write that question outside the notice. This keeps an unresolved planning issue from becoming a public instruction. Your completed worksheet should contain the source facts, two styles, the imperfect revision, the correction and the missing-information question. These are practical evidence of a controlled writing task rather than evidence about any actual assistant's performance.

### Explained version

A prompt should say what work to do, who will read the result, which facts to use and what format you need. Make the requirements easy to check.

Use the library fact sheet: Saturday, 10:00, free entry, optional registration. If a mock response invents a venue or makes registration compulsory, correct it. If the venue is needed, ask for it instead of guessing.

A style example can show short sentences, but its dates and names are not facts for your new message. Mark the example's purpose clearly.

You can compare two invented assistant responses offline. Give both the same request and check factual agreement, required details, format and unsupported additions. This teaches a comparison method; it does not rank real ChatGPT or Gemini services.

Practise with fictional information. Keep a first prompt, a revised prompt, two mock answers and a correction record. The aim is to request useful work and show how you checked it.

## 3. Build a source-grounded study note

## Begin with material you can inspect

A useful study note starts with evidence. A source-grounded notebook works with material supplied to it rather than asking you to treat every generated sentence as an established fact. Google's current notebook documentation describes a sources panel, questions about those sources and outputs such as notes and study guides. The playlist calls this topic Notebook LM. Names and features may change, so the required activity here uses a paper notebook and invented source cards instead of a live product.

Your goal is to build a short note whose important claims can be traced to supplied material. You will practise identifying sources, separating agreement from conflict and returning questions when evidence is missing. You do not need to upload a file, share a document or open an external account. The evidence is included in the exercise, and you can keep it beside your work throughout.

## Create three fictional source cards

Write Card One: the library reading session begins at 10:00 on Saturday; entry is free; registration is optional. Write Card Two: the session includes a welcome, a reading activity and a closing discussion. Write Card Three: an earlier planning note proposed Sunday at 11:00, but it was a draft and was replaced by Card One. Mark the status of each card clearly. These are original teaching examples, not real event announcements.

Give each card a short identifier and a description of what it supports. Card One supports the current date, time and entry conditions. Card Two supports the activity sequence. Card Three supports the history of a proposal, not the final event arrangements. Keeping the older card can help explain a conflict, provided that you do not silently treat it as equally current.

## Map claims to evidence

Write a draft study note of four sentences describing the event. Beside each sentence, record which card supports it. If you cannot name a supporting card, classify the sentence as an unsupported addition or a question. This is a practical way to review your own summary. It also makes your reasoning visible to another person without requiring them to trust your confidence.

Now add two deliberately problematic claims: snacks are provided, and everyone must register. The first is absent from the supplied cards. The second conflicts with Card One. Correct the note using the cards, then describe the difference between missing evidence and conflicting evidence. A source list alone would not solve either problem if you never compared the wording of the claims with the content of the sources.

## Handle a disagreement explicitly

Suppose you receive Card Four, a later message saying the time may change but giving no confirmed replacement. You now have a reason to investigate the time. Do not invent a new time or decide that a longer message is automatically more reliable. Write a question asking which arrangement is currently confirmed. Keep the status of the unresolved detail visible in your working note.

For practice, produce two versions of the note. The first is an internal draft that records the unresolved time question. The second is a distribution plan that says which information must be confirmed before the notice is shared. You are not publishing anything in this exercise. You are demonstrating how to avoid turning incomplete planning information into an apparently final statement.

## Separate quotation, paraphrase and your own reasoning

A quotation copies wording from a source. A paraphrase expresses a source's meaning in new wording. Your own reasoning adds a conclusion or recommendation that you have developed. In this exercise, the source cards are original material supplied for practice. You can copy a short sentence to show its exact wording, but keep it visibly separate from your commentary.

Paraphrase the optional-registration sentence without changing its meaning. Then write your own recommendation that the organiser should confirm the time before distribution. Label that recommendation as your review decision. It is supported by the unresolved conflict, but it is not a sentence that appeared in the original event card. This distinction helps a reader understand where your source ends and your analysis begins.

## Ask questions the evidence can answer

Write three questions whose answers appear in the cards, such as which activities are planned or whether entry costs money. Add two questions whose answers are absent, such as the confirmed venue or the number of seats. Answer the first group with a source identifier. For the second group, write not supplied and propose a person or document that could resolve the gap. Use fictional roles rather than real personal contact details.

Next, revise one question that is too broad. Instead of asking whether the event is excellent, ask whether the cards provide a confirmed time and activity sequence. The narrower question is easier to answer with the available material. It does not claim to assess every quality of the event or predict how participants will respond.

## Create a study guide independently

Invent a different low-stakes topic, such as organising a book exchange or preparing a classroom display. Write three short source cards with statuses and identifiers. Include one detail that appears in two cards and one detail that is deliberately missing. Build a brief note, three study questions and a list of unresolved information. Use original fictional content rather than copying a private message or a long published passage.

Ask a partner to check one important claim using your source identifiers, or perform that check yourself. Record the result beside the claim. If your identifier leads to a card that discusses a different topic, correct the reference. The aim is not to collect the largest possible pile of sources; it is to keep the evidence relevant and easy to inspect.

## Recap and preserve the trail

Your evidence should include the source cards, claim map, corrected note, questions and unresolved-information list. Explain why a generated summary needs checking even when it comes from a notebook organised around supplied sources. State which claims are confirmed in the exercise, which are disputed and which are not supplied. Keep the older planning card's status visible. This gives you a reviewable study note and prepares you to turn reliable information into presentations and scripts in the next lessons.

## Trace an inventory calculation to its inputs

Create another fictional source set. Card A records forty books in a cupboard at the start of Monday. Card B records five of those books lent out on Tuesday. Card C says ten donated books are promised for Friday but have not yet arrived. Assume no other movement has occurred by Wednesday. Under these stated exercise conditions, the Wednesday inventory is forty minus five, or thirty-five. A future promise is not an observed addition.

Write a short inventory note and mark the cards supporting each part. Describe the promised donation separately. Now create an imperfect note that counts the ten promised books as already received. Correct it by checking both the number and its status. This illustrates why summarising records requires more than copying all the numbers into a single total.

Next, remove the statement that no other movement occurred. Explain what uncertainty appears in the calculation. You can still report the recorded starting count and recorded loan, but you should identify the assumption behind a current-total claim. Ask for any additional movement records before presenting a confirmed inventory. Keep the exact exercise conditions visible beside the arithmetic.

Finally, write two questions: one about the recorded Tuesday loan, and one about whether the donation arrived. Answer the first using Card B. Mark the second as not established by Card C. Keep your source cards, calculation, corrected note and unresolved question. They demonstrate how dates, statuses and assumptions affect an otherwise simple summary.

### Explained version

Keep your sources beside your summary. Give each source an identifier and say whether it is current, draft or replaced.

In the exercise, Card One supplies the confirmed event facts. Card Two supplies the activity sequence. Card Three is an older proposal, so it does not replace the current arrangements. Write a source identifier beside each important claim in your note.

A snack claim has no evidence. Compulsory registration conflicts with the supplied optional-registration fact. These are different problems: one detail is missing, and another disagrees with the evidence. Correct both. If a later card creates uncertainty about the time, ask for confirmation instead of inventing a replacement.

Keep exact wording, paraphrases and your own recommendations distinguishable. Write questions the cards can answer, and mark other answers as not supplied.

Finish with source cards, a claim map, a corrected note and an unresolved-information list. You can do the whole task on paper. It teaches source review without requiring a notebook account or pretending that a generated summary has already been verified.

## 4. Turn checked facts into a presentation

## Plan what the audience needs

A presentation is a sequence of messages for an audience. Before asking an assistant to draft slides, decide what the audience should understand or do. This lesson uses paper slide cards. It connects to the playlist's presentation topic without requiring an online design service, a paid template or generated images. You will create a compact outline, write speaker notes and check the claims against supplied evidence.

Imagine that a fictional library wants volunteers to help with a book exchange. Your audience is an adult who may have only a few minutes to decide whether to take part. Write a one-sentence purpose: explain the event and the help requested. Avoid adding promises about jobs, qualifications or rewards. The exercise provides no evidence for those claims, and they are not needed to communicate the event plan.

## Prepare a small fact sheet

Use these original fictional facts: the exchange takes place on Saturday at 10:00; entry is free; visitors may bring up to two books; three activities are planned, consisting of a welcome, an exchange and a closing discussion. The organiser still needs to confirm the venue. There are no supplied statistics about attendance or outcomes. Mark the facts as exercise data so your work cannot be mistaken for a real public announcement.

Give each fact an identifier. Write a separate line for the missing venue. This sheet is the boundary of your presentation's factual content. If you want a decorative example, label it as an illustration. Do not treat an image of a large crowd as evidence that many people have registered. Your outline should communicate what is known without making the event appear more certain or successful than the source material supports.

## Build four paper slide cards

Use the first card to state the event's purpose. Use the second for the confirmed arrangements. Use the third for what a visitor may bring and how the activity sequence works. Use the fourth for questions and the information still awaiting confirmation. This is an original exercise structure, not a universal rule that every presentation must contain four slides. You may revise the number if your own later task needs a different sequence.

Write a short heading on each card and choose only the facts needed for that message. Do not copy the entire fact sheet onto every card. In a separate speaker-note area, explain what you would say aloud. This separation lets you keep the visible message manageable while retaining the explanation needed by someone presenting it.

## Use a prompt as a planning aid

A request for an outline can specify the audience, purpose, supplied facts and desired format. On paper, write a prompt asking for four slide headings with two brief points each and one speaker note per slide. Add the instruction that missing information should be returned as a question. You are applying the prompting method from the earlier lesson to a new output format.

Create an invented assistant response that includes a venue, an attendance total and a certificate claim. Compare those additions with your fact sheet. None is supplied. Remove them and record the corrections. Then write a second response that follows the request. Keeping both versions shows how your review changed the result, which is more useful evidence than simply declaring the final outline excellent.

## Check numbers and conditions

Inspect the phrase up to two books. A card saying bring exactly two books changes the condition. A card saying bring any number changes it in another way. Rewrite the requirement so a reader can act on the supplied rule. Next, check the time and free-entry statement. Keep each number attached to the item it describes, rather than leaving a bare number that could be misunderstood.

Do a small consistency audit across the four cards and their speaker notes. If the first card says Saturday but a note says Sunday, identify the conflict before presenting. If a note supplies a new figure, ask which evidence supports it. The review should examine both visible slides and spoken material because an unsupported claim can enter either part of the presentation.

## Make the outline usable without extra media

Read the cards in order without relying on an image, animation or audio clip. Can another person understand the purpose, arrangements and visitor action from the words? For the exercise, use short sentences and define any unusual term. If a visual would help, sketch a simple original diagram and write a text description of what it communicates. The core information should still be available in the notes.

Choose one card and explain it to a partner, or record your explanation in writing. Ask what action the reader thinks is required. If their interpretation differs from the fact sheet, revise the wording. This feedback does not measure a population or prove that every reader will understand; it gives you one useful check on your own material.

## Practise a different outline

Create a second fictional fact sheet for a low-stakes topic such as a garden swap, reading circle or classroom display. Include a purpose, two confirmed arrangements, one visitor action and one unresolved detail. Choose a suitable sequence of paper slide cards. You do not need to preserve the four-card format if another sequence better fits this new material. Explain your choice.

Write a request for the outline, a mock response and your corrections. Include one deliberately ambiguous condition so you can demonstrate how you clarified it. Keep the facts separate from your design decisions: a heading or order is your choice, while a time or quantity needs evidence. This distinction allows creativity without weakening the factual content.

## Recap and retain the presentation pack

Keep the fact sheet, outline, speaker notes, mock response and correction log together. Explain one improvement you made to clarity and one unsupported addition you removed. State what must be confirmed before the fictional notice could be distributed. You have prepared a presentation that another reader can review without a live AI service. Its quality comes from a clear purpose, relevant evidence and deliberate checking, rather than from a tool name or a claim that generated slides are automatically accurate.

## Review an audience interpretation

For a second presentation exercise, imagine a book display with these fictional conditions: visitors may look at the books without registration, but borrowing is available only to registered members. No membership fee or registration procedure is supplied. Prepare two cards explaining the difference between looking and borrowing. Write a speaker note that preserves the condition without inventing how someone becomes a member.
